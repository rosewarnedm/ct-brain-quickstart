# Self-Assessment Answers

Answers to the FRCR-style self-assessment questions at the end of each chapter. Chapters 1–4 use multiple true/false (T = true, F = false for each stem a–e); Chapters 5–26 use single best answer (the correct option letter is given).


### Chapter 1: CT Physics and Technique for Acute Brain CT: A Practical Guide for ED Clinicians

**Q1.** (a) T (b) T (c) T (d) F (e) F — Water is 0 HU and air −1000 HU by definition; grey–white differs by only 5–10 HU; acute clot is 50–80 HU. A focus of 250 HU is almost certainly calcification or dense iodine, not blood (blood rarely exceeds ~80 HU). Fat is negative (−30 to −100 HU).

**Q2.** (a) T (b) T (c) T (d) F (e) T — Subacute haematoma (30–40 HU) can be isodense and is a classic miss; chronic haematoma falls to 0–20 HU. Mass effect and sulcal effacement flag an isodense subdural. Attenuation *falls* rather than rises as a haematoma ages. Fresh clot is hyperdense due to high protein and packed red cells.

**Q3.** (a) T (b) T (c) F (d) T (e) T — Standard brain windows are WL ~35 / WW ~80; posterior fossa needs a wider width (~160–200) to reduce beam hardening. Bone windows are for fractures/calcification, not SAH (blood/SAH windows are used for subtle subarachnoid blood). Partial volume averaging can hide thin subdurals and hairline fractures on thick slices; blood windows reveal subtle SAH.

**Q4.** (a) T (b) F (c) T (d) T (e) T — Petrous beam hardening mimics/obscures posterior fossa lesions. In diffuse cerebral oedema the parenchyma becomes globally *hypodense*, making normal vessels/tentorium look relatively hyperdense (pseudo-SAH). The dense tentorium can mimic SAH on thick slices; polycythaemia/dehydration cause bilateral vessel hyperdensity without thrombosis; motion artefact gives blurring/double contours and may need a limited repeat.

**Q5.** (a) T (b) F (c) T (d) T (e) F — Contrast staining after reperfusion therapy mimics haemorrhage. On delayed NCCT the relationship is reversed: iodine staining *fades* while true haemorrhage persists or increases. On DECT haemorrhage persists on the virtual non-contrast image but iodine staining disappears. Acute DVST gives a hyperdense "dense sinus sign" (~60–75 HU). Fetal dose from maternal head CT is negligible (shield abdomen); head CT is not contraindicated in pregnancy.

### Chapter 2: Normal Brain Anatomy on CT

**Q1.** (a) F (b) T (c) F (d) T (e) T — Grey matter is slightly *higher* attenuation than white matter (~35–45 vs 20–30 HU); loss of this contrast is abnormal until proved otherwise, so (a) is false. Acute clotted blood is ~60–80 HU. Calcification (>100 HU, often 150–400+) is *denser* than acute haemorrhage, making (c) false — this difference is used to distinguish them. CSF is 0–15 HU, and denser blood (higher haematocrit/dehydration/polycythaemia) can mimic thrombus.

**Q2.** (a) F (b) T (c) T (d) F (e) T — Isodense and interhemispheric/extra-axial subdurals are best seen on the *subdural* window (~50/200), not bone, so (a) is false. Beam hardening from petrous bones creates artefactual posterior fossa low attenuation. Rotation/tilt causes pseudo-asymmetry and pseudo-shift. Thick 5 mm slices *increase* the risk of missing small bleeds/subdurals through partial volume averaging, so (d) is false. Recent contrast or angiography makes sinuses and choroid plexus look dense.

**Q3.** (a) T (b) T (c) F (d) T (e) F — Evans index <0.30 is normal; normal temporal horns are ≤2 mm and barely visible. Cavum septi pellucidi is a benign normal variant when isolated, so (c) is false. Effaced basal cisterns signal raised ICP. In pseudo-SAH (diffuse oedema) the ventricles are typically *small or slit-like*, not dilated, so (e) is false.

**Q4.** (a) T (b) T (c) F (d) T (e) F — Loss of grey–white differentiation is the earliest ischaemic sign, with the insular ribbon and lentiform nucleus most sensitive for early MCA infarct. The internal capsule is *hypodense* (darker) relative to adjacent grey nuclei, so (c) is false. The putamen and globus pallidus form the lentiform nucleus. Globus pallidus calcification is *common* in adults >40 years and should not be mistaken for haemorrhage, so (e) is false.

**Q5.** (a) F (b) T (c) T (d) T (e) T — Thrombotic hyperdense artery is focal/*unilateral* with parenchymal correlates; bilateral symmetric density suggests physiological high haematocrit/dehydration, so (a) is false. Arachnoid granulations are normal CSF-density non-expansile filling defects. The cord sign indicates straight sinus thrombosis. Choroid plexus calcification is bilateral at the atria, >100 HU. Physiological GP calcification is punctate, bilateral, symmetric with no oedema.

### Chapter 3: A Systematic Approach to Reading Acute Brain CT

**Q1.** (a) T (b) T (c) F (d) T (e) F — Acute clot is 50–80 HU and CSF 0–15 HU; ischaemia is hypodense (cells swell with water), and calcification (80–400 HU) is far denser than grey matter (30–45 HU).
**Q2.** (a) T (b) T (c) F (d) T (e) T — The blood window uses a wider width (WW 130–200) than brain (WW 80); work through the checklist in order rather than jumping to the suspected lesion; posterior fossa beam hardening and effaced cisterns are both key teaching points.
**Q3.** (a) T (b) T (c) T (d) F (e) T — EDH is biconvex and suture-limited; SDH is crescentic, crosses sutures and follows dural reflections; the isodense phase is the most dangerous (easiest to miss), not the safest.
**Q4.** (a) T (b) F (c) T (d) T (e) T — CT sensitivity for SAH exceeds 95% in the first six hours; a perimesencephalic-only pattern is usually a benign venous pattern (still needs CTA), not aneurysmal; the remaining stems reflect standard aetiology and escalation rules.
**Q5.** (a) T (b) T (c) F (d) T (e) T — Insular ribbon loss and ASPECTS starting at 10 are core early-ischaemia teaching; only acute change is scored (do not penalise old infarcts); a hyperdense sinus >60–70 HU suggests thrombosis, but high haematocrit/dehydration is a recognised pitfall causing uniformly dense venous blood.

### Chapter 4: The Developing and Ageing Brain, Normal Variants and Common Background Pathology

**Q1.** (a) T (b) F (c) T (d) F (e) T — Neonatal unmyelinated white matter is water-rich and low attenuation; myelination progresses deep-to-superficial and brainstem outward (not superficial-to-deep); in BESSI cortical veins traverse the enlarged space rather than being displaced inward (unlike subdural collections), and it peaks at 6–12 months in macrocephalic infants.

**Q2.** (a) T (b) T (c) F (d) T (e) T — CSP sits between the frontal horns anterior to the foramina of Monro, with cavum vergae as its posterior extension above the third ventricle; mega cisterna magna has a normal vermis and fourth ventricle (vermian hypoplasia/enlarged fourth ventricle points to Dandy–Walker); mild ventricular asymmetry (often left larger) and small perivascular spaces are common normal variants.

**Q3.** (a) T (b) T (c) T (d) T (e) F — Involutional (ex vacuo) change gives sharp-margined ventricles without periventricular oedema; NPH shows disproportionate ventricular enlargement with the tight-convexity DESH pattern; globus pallidus is the commonest physiological basal ganglia calcification site; young/asymmetric/extensive calcification prompts a metabolic screen; calcification versus subdural must be confirmed on bone windows, not narrow brain windows.

**Q4.** (a) T (b) T (c) T (d) F (e) T — Chronic SVD is symmetric periventricular low attenuation (caps and bands) with U-fibres spared until advanced, and its severity affects thrombolysis risk; a chronic lacune is a small CSF-density cavity, whereas an acute lacunar infarct is often CT-occult (isodense/subtly hypodense, no cavitation) and may need MRI diffusion.

**Q5.** (a) F (b) T (c) T (d) F (e) T — Meningioma is extra-axial with a broad dural base and obtuse angle to the skull (not acute-angled or intra-axial); pineal cysts show peripheral calcification displaced outward; arachnoid cysts are extra-axial CSF-density lesions that may scallop bone and predispose to subdural bleeding; low-lying tonsils are not herniation without global mass effect/cisternal effacement; Chiari I is ≥5 mm tonsillar descent in a symptomatic adult.

### Chapter 5: Extradural Haematoma

**Q1.** (b) — Failure to cross suture lines is the single most useful distinguishing feature of an EDH; the periosteal dura is tethered at sutures, so an arterial EDH stops there whereas an SDH spreads across them. Hyperdensity (a) and mass effect (d) are common to both acute EDH and acute SDH. Not crossing the falx (c) is true of EDH but is also true of SDH, so it does not discriminate. A young patient after direct trauma (e) is the typical demographic but is only supportive, not the strongest single feature.

**Q2.** (c) — The swirl sign — hypodense (non-clotted) foci within an otherwise hyperdense clot — indicates active ongoing haemorrhage and a high risk of rapid expansion, mandating urgent escalation. It is not a marker of subacute (a) or chronic (b) evolution, which show generalised isodensity or hypodensity. It is intracranial, not a scalp collection (d), and must never be dismissed as artefact (e).

**Q3.** (d) — Any neurological deterioration (falling GCS, new focal deficit, anisocoria) plus a large volume and significant shift are indications for immediate neurosurgical contact; the reader's role is to recognise and escalate to the team that performs decompression. Repeat imaging (a) or conservative management/observation (b, e) are inappropriate given active deterioration and would dangerously delay treatment. CTV (c) may be relevant for sinus-related EDH but must not delay escalation in an acutely deteriorating patient.

**Q4.** (b) — EDH may cross a diastatic suture in young children, an important exception to the "does not cross sutures" rule, so suture crossing here does not exclude EDH. It does not force a diagnosis of SDH (a) or exclude a surgical lesion (d). The biconvex acute appearance is not chronic (c), and it is a real finding, not artefact (e).

**Q5.** (c) — CT venography is recommended when an EDH abuts a dural venous sinus (transverse, sigmoid, superior sagittal) to assess for sinus injury or thrombosis, which significantly changes operative strategy. Spine CT (a), skull radiographs (b), chest CT (d), and a plain thin-slice repeat head CT (e) do not evaluate venous sinus patency.

### Chapter 6: Subdural Haematoma

**Q1.** (c) — The collection meets neurosurgical thresholds (thickness >10 mm, midline shift >5 mm, and basal cistern effacement suggesting impending herniation), so immediate neurosurgical referral is mandated; the warfarin should trigger urgent haematology-guided reversal. It is hyperdense and acute, not chronic (a). MRI (b) delays escalation and is not the priority here. The CT reader recognises and refers — they do not operate personally (d). Routine follow-up (e) is unsafe.
**Q2.** (b) — The single most useful discriminators are that an SDH crosses suture lines (an EDH does not) and an SDH is stopped by dural reflections (an EDH may cross them). Option (a) reverses the shapes (SDH is crescentic, EDH biconvex). Fracture association is more consistent with EDH, not invariable with SDH (c). Acute SDH is hyperdense, not hypodense (d). It is the SDH, not the EDH, that displaces cortical veins away from the skull (e).
**Q3.** (a) — At 5 days a subdural becomes early subacute and may be isodense to grey matter (~30–45 HU), making it invisible on standard windows — the classic trap flagged by unilateral sulcal effacement and grey–white buckling. Chronic near-CSF density (c) and pure CSF hygroma (e) would appear hypodense; acute blood (b) would be conspicuously hyperdense; calcified tentorium (d) is >100 HU and does not explain a convexity collection.
**Q4.** (d) — The cortical vein sign distinguishes the two: in cerebral atrophy veins course through the widened subarachnoid space and abut the inner table, whereas in a subdural collection the veins are displaced medially away from the skull. Visible veins within the fluid abutting the skull therefore indicate atrophy, not an SDH (a, b, c) or EDH (e).
**Q5.** (e) — Bilateral or interhemispheric subdural collections in a young child with an inconsistent or absent history of trauma require immediate safeguarding escalation for possible non-accidental injury; this must not be delayed. Dismissing the findings (a), routine outpatient management (b), assuming intracranial hypotension (c), or focusing solely on drainage (d) all fail the safeguarding imperative.

### Chapter 7: Traumatic Subarachnoid Haemorrhage (tSAH)

**Q1.** (b) — A peripheral, sulcal-predominant distribution adjacent to a cortical contusion, anchored to an external sign of impact (scalp haematoma), is the archetypal pattern of tSAH and confirms a traumatic aetiology. (a) IVH and (d) symmetrical Sylvian filling are features that instead raise concern for aneurysmal SAH; (c) attenuation above 45 HU confirms the material is blood but says nothing about its cause; (e) temporal horn dilatation suggests hydrocephalus, again a red flag rather than reassurance of trauma.

**Q2.** (c) — Dense, star-shaped, central cisternal-dominant blood with minimal traumatic findings is the red-flag pattern for aneurysmal SAH; the CT reader recognises this and refers by recommending urgent CTA, since a pre-existing aneurysm can rupture as the cause of the fall. (a) wrongly attributes the pattern to trauma; (b) nimodipine is not the radiologist's call and is not standard for isolated tSAH; (d) amyloid angiopathy gives convexity, not central cisternal, blood; (e) pseudo-SAH is lower attenuation (typically 20–40 HU) and lacks discrete sulcal/cisternal moulding.

**Q3.** (b) — Thin, peripheral tSAH commonly clears from CT within about 7–14 days, so absence of visible blood at day 10 is expected and the earlier scan remains the reference for the acute finding. (a) is wrong because tSAH does resolve; (c) resolution does not imply an aneurysmal source; (d) clearance alone does not mandate CTA; (e) tSAH does not "convert" into a chronic subdural collection.

**Q4.** (c) — The reporting radiologist recognises the need and refers: promptly flag the anticoagulation and haemorrhage to the admitting team so urgent reversal (with haematology as needed) can be arranged, and comment on stability, which informs thromboprophylaxis timing. (a) and (d)/(b)/(e) all describe prescribing or definitive treatment decisions that sit with the receiving/treating team, not the radiologist; starting thromboprophylaxis immediately is also inappropriate given many centres repeat CT at 6–24 hours first.

**Q5.** (c) — Material that is sharply marginated, over 100 HU, lying along the falx and unchanged on prior imaging is calcification, not acute blood. (a) 55 HU material moulding to a sulcus and (b) asymmetric sulcal density both favour true SAH; (d) blood tracking within sulci adjacent to a contusion is classic tSAH; (e) dependent occipital-horn layering indicates intraventricular haemorrhage, i.e. real blood.

### Chapter 8: Contusions and Diffuse Axonal Injury

**Q1.** (c) — Contusions favour the anteroinferior frontal lobes (orbitofrontal cortex) and temporal poles because the irregular skull base funnels impact energy there. In contrecoup injury the lesion lies opposite the site of impact, so an occipital scalp haematoma should make you scrutinise the frontal poles hard. (a) is the coup site, not where contrecoup contusions concentrate. (b) is the distribution of hypertensive haemorrhage, not typical contusions. (d) watershed cortex is an ischaemic pattern. (e) cerebellar hemispheres are not classic contusion sites.

**Q2.** (a) — Enlargement and coalescence of haemorrhagic contusions with increasing oedema and mass effect over the first 24–72 hours is expected "blossoming", not a complication — though it can cause deterioration and cross surgical thresholds. (b) aneurysmal rebleed does not fit a focal contusion evolving in this pattern. (c) abscess develops over days-to-weeks with ring enhancement, not at 48 hours post-trauma. (d) is a non-contrast trauma scan, so contrast extravasation is irrelevant. (e) haemorrhagic transformation of infarction requires an arterial territory infarct, not a traumatic contusion.

**Q3.** (e) — A burst lobe (large heterogeneous lobar haemorrhagic contusion, significant mass effect, overlying acute subdural) is a surgical pattern; the CT reader recognises it and refers urgently to neurosurgery for consideration of surgical management. (a) and (b) dangerously delay definitive care. (c) is false — it is not self-limiting. (d) CTA is not the priority; the appearance is characteristic of trauma and delaying surgical discussion for angiography is inappropriate.

**Q4.** (b) — A normal CT does not exclude diffuse axonal injury; unexplained coma disproportionate to CT findings is DAI until proven otherwise. The correct step is to refer to critical care/anaesthetics for intensive monitoring and airway protection and arrange urgent MRI (SWI/DWI) once stable. (a) is the dangerous misconception the chapter warns against. (c) assumes intoxication without basis. (d) craniectomy is not indicated for a normal CT with no mass lesion. (e) LP is contraindicated/irrelevant here and does not address DAI.

**Q5.** (d) — In the Adams classification, dorsolateral brainstem involvement is Grade III, the most severe pattern, correlating with the worst prognosis (Grade I = grey–white junction, Grade II = additional corpus callosum). Brainstem foci warrant referral to critical care/anaesthetics for ICU-level monitoring. (a) and (b) understate the grade and ignore the brainstem foci. (c) describes Grade II. (e) is incorrect — the stem describes brainstem involvement, not sparing.

### Chapter 9: Skull Fractures and Base of Skull Injury

**Q1.** (b) — A fracture crossing the middle meningeal artery groove along the inner table of the temporoparietal bone carries a high risk of epidural haematoma, so the adjacent epidural space must be scrutinised. (a) The sagittal sinus groove risks venous EDH/sinus injury but is not the temporoparietal MMA territory described. (c) Cribriform involvement risks CSF rhinorrhoea/anosmia, not EDH. (d) Carotid canal involvement risks blunt cerebrovascular injury. (e) Otic capsule violation causes sensorineural hearing loss, not EDH.

**Q2.** (c) — Tiny −1000 HU air foci within the cochlea/vestibule constitute pneumolabyrinth, which is pathognomonic of otic capsule violation (an OCV temporal bone fracture) and predicts profound SNHL and vertigo. (a) Haemotympanum is 30–60 HU blood in the middle ear, not intralabyrinthine air. (b) Haematosinus is 50–90 HU blood in a sinus. (d) Pneumocephalus is intracranial (not intralabyrinthine) air. (e) The Mount Fuji sign describes bilateral frontal air causing tension pneumocephalus.

**Q3.** (d) — Carotid canal involvement mandates a low threshold for CTA to exclude blunt cerebrovascular injury (dissection, pseudoaneurysm, carotid–cavernous fistula), with referral to neurosurgery/vascular. (a) Stable neurology does not exclude BCVI, which is under-recognised. (b) HRCT ossicles is for suspected temporal bone/ossicular injury, not the carotid canal. (c) Cisternography is for localising a CSF leak. (e) The radiologist recognises and refers; antithrombotic decisions are made by the treating team guided by Biffl grade and protocol.

**Q4.** (b) — An Anderson–Montesano type III occipital condyle fracture is an alar ligament avulsion that is potentially unstable; maintain cervical immobilisation, refer to the spine service (neurosurgery/spinal), and request MRI for ligamentous assessment. (a) Type III is not stable. (c) ENT facial nerve exploration relates to temporal bone facial palsy, not condyle fractures. (d) Conservative oxygen therapy applies to simple pneumocephalus. (e) Cisternography localises CSF leaks, not relevant here.

**Q5.** (c) — A widening, scalloped-edged calvarial defect developing over weeks to months at a prior diastatic fracture in a young child is a growing skull fracture (leptomeningeal cyst) from a dural tear allowing arachnoid/brain herniation; it requires neurosurgical referral. (a) Accessory sutures are symmetric with sclerotic margins and do not enlarge. (b) The spheno-occipital synchondrosis is a smooth, corticated skull-base line, not an enlarging vault defect. (d) Metastasis is unexpected in a toddler and unrelated to the documented trauma site. (e) Arachnoid granulations are smooth CSF-density defects within a venous sinus, not an enlarging vault defect.

### Chapter 10: Paediatric Head Injury

**Q1.** (b) — The radiologist's role is to describe findings accurately and objectively and to escalate; the safeguarding conclusion is a multidisciplinary decision, so (a) overreaches the reader's role. Bilateral SDH, interhemispheric blood and loss of grey–white differentiation are concerning features that mandate urgent escalation to the safeguarding/child-protection team and named/on-call paediatric consultant. (c) is wrong because birth-related SDH is thin, tentorial/posterior interhemispheric and typically resolves by 2–3 months — it does not explain bilateral convexity collections with encephalopathy. (d) is unsafe: CT alone is insufficient for AHT and MRI brain and whole spine are needed. (e) is inappropriate and not indicated here.

**Q2.** (c) — The swirl sign (hypodense areas within a hyperdense clot) indicates active, unclotted bleeding and signals a surgical emergency. The biconvex shape (a), an overlying fracture (b, present in ~70–90% of EDH) and failure to cross sutures (d) are characteristic descriptive features of EDH but do not by themselves denote an emergency. Temporoparietal location (e) is common but not the key emergency feature.

**Q3.** (b) — In BESS the cortical veins traverse (cross) the widened fluid space — the "cortical vein sign." In SDH the cortical veins are displaced and compressed against the brain surface (a). Mixed density (c), midline shift with sulcal effacement (d), and a crescentic collection conforming to the brain surface (e) all favour SDH. Note that BESS does not exclude AHT and any infant SDH still warrants thorough evaluation.

**Q4.** (d) — Diffuse cerebral oedema (diffusely low-attenuation brain, slit ventricles, effaced cisterns) causes pseudo-SAH: engorged basal veins look relatively hyperdense against the swollen low-attenuation brain. This is a sign of critically raised ICP requiring urgent escalation to neurosurgery and paediatric critical care. (a) is the classic pitfall — do not request CTA/LP for aneurysm on pseudo-SAH alone. (b) is wrong; there is no crescentic hypodense collection. (c) is unsafe; the study is grossly abnormal. (e) misapplies the choroid plexus pitfall, which concerns IVH not the basal cisterns.

**Q5.** (c) — The clinical severity greatly exceeds the CT findings and the punctate foci at grey–white junctions and the splenium are stigmata of diffuse axonal injury, for which CT is insensitive. MRI with SWI and DWI is the investigation of choice. (a) delays diagnosis without adding the needed sensitivity. (b) CTA is for suspected vascular injury/dissection, not DAI. (d) LP is not indicated. (e) skull radiographs do not assess parenchymal axonal injury.

### Chapter 11: Ischaemic Stroke — Early CT Signs, ASPECTS, Large Vessel Occlusion

**Q1.** (c) — A clearly hyperdense M1 (>50 HU and brighter than the contralateral MCA) is a large vessel occlusion surrogate and mandates immediate CTA plus urgent referral to the stroke team / interventional neuroradiology. (a) is wrong because a normal-looking parenchyma does not exclude AIS or LVO — a convincing hyperdense M1 is still an emergency. (b) is wrong: the hyperdense artery is not a contraindication to thrombolysis. (d) is wrong because the reporting radiologist recognises findings and activates the pathway; the stroke physician decides on and delivers thrombolysis. (e) dangerously delays reperfusion in an early-window patient.

**Q2.** (b) — ASPECTS starts at 10 and one point is subtracted for each of the 10 MCA-territory regions showing definite acute hypoattenuation or focal sulcal effacement. (a) reverses the method. (c) is wrong: chronic lacunes and leukoaraiosis must not be scored. (d) is wrong — ASPECTS assesses only the MCA territory, not ACA/PCA. (e) is wrong: 10 is a normal score (no acute change), not extensive infarction.

**Q3.** (a) — Loss of the insular ribbon is frequently the earliest visible parenchymal sign of MCA-territory ischaemia; the insula has a terminal supply with poor collaterals. (b) internal capsule is often undercalled and not the earliest MCA sign. (c) is a PCA-territory finding. (d) M5 and (e) ACA territory are not the earliest MCA-territory changes.

**Q4.** (c) — A focal round hyperdensity in the Sylvian fissure, brighter than the other side and adjacent veins and conforming to the lumen, is the MCA "dot" sign (M2/M3 thrombus) and should be treated as occlusion until CTA proves otherwise. (a) calcification measures >120 HU and is eccentric/visible on bone windows. (b) a normal vein would not be asymmetrically bright relative to other veins. (d) polycythaemia is excluded by the history and would affect vessels symmetrically. (e) haemorrhagic transformation is patchy parenchymal high attenuation within infarcted tissue, not an intraluminal dot.

**Q5.** (b) — The radiologist's role is to recognise findings and promptly provide accurate imaging information (ASPECTS, occlusion site, clot burden, collaterals), arrange CTA and refer urgently; reperfusion decisions are multidisciplinary. (a) is wrong: low ASPECTS alone no longer excludes EVT in many centres (SELECT2, ANGEL-ASPECT, RESCUE-Japan LIMIT). (c) is wrong — the reporting radiologist does not independently decide on or deliver thrombectomy. (d) is wrong: good collaterals can make an early NCCT look deceptively normal despite LVO, and a hyperdense artery must never be disregarded. (e) confuses the IVT window (4.5 h) with EVT, which extends up to 24 h with imaging-based selection.

### Chapter 12: Intracerebral Haemorrhage

**Q1.** (d) — A deep, solitary, homogeneous haematoma centred on the putamen/external capsule with background small vessel change (lacunes, periventricular white matter hypodensity) is the classic picture of hypertensive arteriolopathy. CAA (a) causes lobar/cortical–subcortical bleeds in the elderly, not deep basal ganglia bleeds. AVM (b) and haemorrhagic metastasis (c) are lobar/atypical and would show serpiginous vessels or nodular enhancement and disproportionate oedema. CVT (e) produces non-territorial or parasagittal haemorrhage with a hyperdense sinus/vein.

**Q2.** (b) — The swirl sign is hypodense (unclotted, actively bleeding) blood within the hyperdense clot and is a marker of haemostatic instability and haematoma expansion risk; it should prompt attention to other expansion signs and early escalation/repeat CT. It is not a normal clot appearance (d), not chronic resolution (a), not calcification (c), and does not specifically indicate tumour (e).

**Q3.** (e) — ABC/2 = (A × B × C) ÷ 2 = (4 × 3 × 5) ÷ 2 = 60 ÷ 2 = 30 ml. C = number of slices with haematoma (5) × slice thickness (1 cm) = 5 cm. Answer (c) 60 ml omits the divide-by-two; the others do not follow the formula.

**Q4.** (a) — A cerebellar haematoma >3 cm with fourth ventricular effacement, hydrocephalus, and deteriorating GCS is a neurosurgical emergency; the reader recognises it and refers urgently to neurosurgery for evacuation and/or CSF diversion (EVD). Do not wait for further deterioration (d), do not delay with outpatient MRI (b), and anticoagulation (c) is contraindicated. A delayed post-contrast CT (e) wastes critical time.

**Q5.** (c) — Fluid–fluid (haematocrit) levels and known anticoagulation should be flagged explicitly to prompt urgent anticoagulation reversal (may be ED-initiated per protocol) with haematology/pharmacy involvement. Anticoagulation is highly relevant (b, d are wrong). There is no aneurysm to clip here (a), and thrombolysis (e) is contraindicated in intracerebral haemorrhage.

### Chapter 13: Subarachnoid Haemorrhage

**Q1.** (c) — Dense blood in the basal cisterns in a "star" configuration with Sylvian extension is aneurysmal SAH until proven otherwise. The correct reader/ED action is to arrange CTA in the same sitting to identify the culprit aneurysm and refer urgently to neurosurgery (and neurointerventional/INR) who secure it. Discharge (a) is dangerous with obvious SAH; LP (b) is for a CT-negative case, not needed when SAH is already seen on CT; outpatient MRI (d) is too slow and inappropriate for acute aneurysmal SAH; CTV (e) is reserved for suspected CVST (typically convexity SAH), not a basal-cistern aneurysmal pattern.

**Q2.** (a) — Blood confined to the prepontine/interpeduncular cisterns with no Sylvian, suprasellar, interhemispheric or intraventricular extension is the classic perimesencephalic non-aneurysmal pattern, which generally carries a low risk of rebleeding, vasospasm and hydrocephalus. However CTA is still required to exclude an aneurysm before applying the label, so (b) is wrong. It is not diagnostic of an ACom aneurysm (c) — ACom bleeds anteriorly into the interhemispheric/suprasellar region. It is not traumatic (d), which produces thin convexity sulcal blood, nor CAA (e), which causes convexity SAH in the elderly.

**Q3.** (b) — Enlarging rounded temporal horns (earliest sign) with periventricular low attenuation and a falling GCS indicate acute obstructive hydrocephalus, a neurosurgical emergency requiring urgent referral for an external ventricular drain (CSF diversion). Mannitol (a) does not treat obstructive hydrocephalus and delays definitive care. CT perfusion (c) is used in the vasospasm window (days 3–14), not for acute hydrocephalus. DSA (d) addresses the aneurysm, not the deteriorating conscious level from CSF obstruction. Anticoagulation (e) is inappropriate and dangerous in acute haemorrhage.

**Q4.** (c) — Isolated convexity sulcal SAH with clear basal cisterns in an elderly patient should first raise cerebral amyloid angiopathy (look for superficial siderosis on MRI). ### Chapter 16: Meningitis and Encephalitis on CT

**Q1.** (c) — Time-critical empirical antibiotics must never be delayed for CT or LP; the chapter stresses that a normal CT never excludes CNS infection and imaging must not delay treatment. **Q5.** (d) — Dependent hyperattenuating debris (~28 HU, above CSF) in the occipital horns with irregular CSF, periventricular transependymal low attenuation and ependymal enhancement is the classic picture of ventriculitis. ### Chapter 18: Hydrocephalus

**Q1.** (b) — Prominent, widened sulci at the high convexities favour atrophy; in hydrocephalus the high-convexity sulci are effaced/crowded. (a) A smooth continuous low-attenuation rim is transependymal oedema, which points to hydrocephalus. (c) Temporal horns enlarged out of proportion and (d) a narrow callosal angle (<80°) both favour (communicating) hydrocephalus. (e) Rounded, ballooned frontal horns are a pressure sign of hydrocephalus, not atrophy.

**Q2.** (d) — Dilated lateral and third ventricles with a normal (small) fourth ventricle is the triventricular pattern of aqueduct of Sylvius obstruction. (a)/(e) Foramen of Monro obstruction enlarges one or both lateral ventricles but not the third. (b) Fourth ventricular outlet obstruction and (c) arachnoid granulation failure produce tetraventricular (all four) enlargement.

**Q3.** (a) — With a posterior fossa mass causing fourth ventricular obstruction, isolated supratentorial drainage can precipitate upward transtentorial herniation; the reader recognises this and flags the risk to neurosurgery before any drainage plan. (b) Bedside supratentorial EVD is the specific hazard to avoid. (c) The obstruction is not self-limiting and carries herniation risk. (d) LP is contraindicated with a posterior fossa mass and obstructive hydrocephalus. (e) Acetazolamide/watchful waiting is inappropriate for acute obstructive hydrocephalus with mass effect.

**Q4.** (e) — Hydrocephalus after SAH can evolve over hours to days, so a single normal early CT does not exclude it; deterioration warrants repeat imaging and, if ventriculomegaly is progressing, urgent neurosurgical referral for CSF diversion (typically EVD). (a) A normal early CT does not exclude evolving hydrocephalus. (b) Deterioration may be from evolving hydrocephalus, not only rebleeding. (c) Communicating hydrocephalus is a well-recognised SAH complication. (d) Ventricular enlargement, not obligatory pre-existing transependymal oedema, is the evolving finding.

**Q5.** (c) — Ventricles larger than the prior post-shunt baseline plus a new transependymal-oedema rim in a symptomatic shunted patient indicate likely shunt failure with re-accumulating hydrocephalus, requiring urgent neurosurgical discussion for shunt assessment/revision. (a) Leukoaraiosis is patchy, non-expansile and not associated with new ventricular enlargement. (b) Overdrainage produces slit-like (small) ventricles with subdural collections, the opposite pattern. (d) New atrophy does not enlarge ventricles over this timescale with transependymal oedema. (e) A catheter tip in parenchyma is malpositioned/abnormal, not the expected location (frontal horn or third ventricle).

### Chapter 19: Cerebral Oedema

**Q1.** (c) — Confluent, finger-like white-matter hypodensity (~18 HU) with cortical sparing and mass effect, in a patient with known malignancy, is the classic appearance of vasogenic oedema from BBB breakdown around metastases. Cytotoxic oedema (a) involves both cortex and deep grey nuclei and follows an arterial territory. Interstitial oedema (b) is periventricular with ventriculomegaly. Osmotic oedema (d) is typically diffuse and often CT-normal. Pseudo-SAH (e) is a sulcal/cisternal hyperattenuation artefact, not white-matter hypodensity.

**Q2.** (e) — A hyperdense MCA (~55–70 HU) represents acute intraluminal thrombus and is the key supporting sign of arterial thromboembolism. Bilateral posterior subcortical hypodensity (a) suggests PRES; periventricular caps (b) indicate interstitial oedema/hydrocephalus; sulcal hyperattenuation ~35 HU (c) suggests pseudo-SAH; temporal horn effacement with callosal bowing (d) points to raised intraventricular pressure — none of these indicate arterial thrombus.

**Q3.** (b) — After anoxic injury, diffuse swelling with a "tight" brain and mildly hyperattenuating sulci/cisterns at ~30–45 HU is pseudo-SAH, caused by venous engorgement and compressed CSF against swollen brain. True SAH (a) measures ~60–70 HU and coats the cisterns uniformly, so CT angiography for an aneurysm is not indicated here. Cord sign (c) is a focal hyperdense cortical vein, not diffuse. Meningeal enhancement (d) requires contrast and a different clinical picture. Beam-hardening (e) is localised to the posterior fossa, not the convexity sulci.

**Q4.** (d) — Bilateral, posterior-predominant, non-territorial subcortical oedema in an eclamptic, severely hypertensive patient is PRES; the atlas action is to escalate to medical/critical care for controlled BP reduction (avoiding precipitous drops) and seizure management, expecting reversibility. Hemicraniectomy (a) is for malignant MCA infarction. Anticoagulation (b) is for CVT. Dexamethasone (c) helps tumoural vasogenic oedema, not PRES. Discharge (e) is unsafe — these are acute, not chronic, changes.

**Q5.** (a) — Malignant MCA infarction with effaced basal cisterns and ≥5 mm midline shift (here 8 mm) at the expected 3–5 day swelling peak warrants urgent neurosurgical discussion for decompressive hemicraniectomy (typically within 48 hours in eligible patients). Steroids (b) are not beneficial in cytotoxic infarct oedema. Routine MRI (c) inappropriately delays care in a deteriorating patient. Fogging (d) occurs at ~2 weeks and does not explain mass effect on day 4. CT venography (e) is for non-territorial/haemorrhagic distributions suggesting CVT, not a territorial arterial infarct.

**Q2.** (d) — A good acute impression names the diagnosis and acuity, quantifies/flags the key complication (early obstructive hydrocephalus with transependymal oedema), and gives a specific next step (urgent neurosurgical referral plus urgent CTA), with the critical result phoned and documented. (a) is equivocal and non-actionable. (b) wrongly omits the mandatory CTA to find the aneurysm. (c) violates the atlas convention — the reporter recommends/refers for definitive action rather than performing it. (e) LP is a clinical decision for equivocal cases, not appropriate when CT already shows unequivocal SAH.

**Q3.** (a) — For suspected stroke the report should give a binary answer on haemorrhage, document the hyperdense vessel sign with HU and side-to-side ratio, state the ASPECTS, and direct the referring team to the stroke pathway. (b) and (c) are equivocal and fail to give the binary haemorrhage answer that drives management. (d) misses the large-vessel occlusion signs. (e) breaks the reporting convention that the reporter recommends action rather than delivering treatment such as thrombolysis.

**Q4.** (e) — A large expanding/large EDH with mass effect and cistern effacement is a time-critical finding: it must be verbally communicated immediately and the time, recipient and content recorded in the report to close the loop, rather than relying on the report being read in time. (a) and (b) rely on the report being read in time. (c) has the reporter performing the definitive intervention, contrary to atlas convention. (d) inappropriately delays escalation of a surgical emergency.

**Q5.** (c) — A subcortical haemorrhage that ignores arterial territories with a hyperdense sinus in a young woman on the OCP is the classic picture of CVST; the report should recommend CTV (or MRV) to confirm and advise anticoagulation unless strongly contraindicated. (a) CT perfusion is for arterial ischaemic penumbra, not venous infarction. (b) nimodipine/BP management is for aneurysmal SAH, not this presentation. (d) hypertensive haemorrhages are deep (basal ganglia/thalamus/pons/cerebellum), not lobar/subcortical, and ignore the hyperdense sinus. (e) dehydration can raise sinus density, but with a matching haemorrhagic venous infarct the finding must be pursued, not dismissed.

### Chapter 26: When to Escalate and What to Do Next

**Q1.** (d) — A large (>30 mL) ICH with midline shift, intraventricular extension and effacing basal cisterns is an operative/CSF-diversion candidate, and the anticoagulation must be reversed urgently; the correct move is simultaneous escalation to neurosurgery and haematology. Outpatient MRI (a) and repeat CT with continued apixaban (c) dangerously delay life-saving intervention. Lumbar puncture (b) is contraindicated with mass effect and impending herniation. Dual-energy CT (e) is for distinguishing contrast from blood, not needed here and should not delay referral.

**Q2.** (a) — This is subarachnoid haemorrhage with acute hydrocephalus (dilated temporal horns, rounded frontal horns, transependymal oedema) and falling GCS, so neurosurgery/critical care must be contacted for a possible EVD while CTA identifies the aneurysm. LP (b) is contraindicated in obstructive hydrocephalus and unnecessary when blood is already visible. The pattern is not benign perimesencephalic SAH (c). Deferring to next-day MRI (d) delays definitive care. Antibiotics for meningitis (e) misreads frank cisternal blood as infection.

**Q3.** (e) — A hyperdense M1 with ASPECTS 8 and no haemorrhage in a patient on the table is a large-vessel-occlusion reperfusion candidate; activate the stroke team and proceed directly to CTA (± CTP) without leaving the scanner. Sending him back for routine MRI (a) wastes the treatment window. Antibiotics/general medicine (b) is inappropriate. Hemicraniectomy (c) is for established malignant MCA infarction, not this acute reperfusion-eligible stage. LP (d) has no role and delays treatment.

**Q4.** (b) — Subdural collections of differing ages with interhemispheric blood and a history inconsistent with the injuries are classic for non-accidental injury, mandating a safeguarding/child-protection referral in parallel with acute neurosurgical care. Discharge (a), routine developmental follow-up (c), reassurance (d) and deferring to an elective MRI (e) all fail the child's safety and are unsafe.

**Q5.** (c) — A crescentic extra-axial collection with disproportionate cortical swelling in a septic patient with sinusitis is a subdural empyema — a surgical emergency requiring urgent neurosurgery and ENT for drainage plus immediate broad-spectrum IV antibiotics. Oral antibiotics with clinic review (a) and deferring for outpatient MRI (d) are dangerously slow. LP (b) is hazardous with mass effect and cortical swelling. Calling it a benign chronic subdural (e) misses a life-threatening infection.
