## CT Reference: Attenuation and Windowing Basics

Keep these values in mind — they underpin almost every distinction in this chapter.

| Structure | HU |
|---|---|
| CSF | 0–15 |
| Unmyelinated white matter (neonate) | 15–20 |
| Myelinated white matter (adult) | 20–30 |
| Grey matter | 35–45 |
| Acute blood | 60–80 |
| Calcification | >100 (often 150–400) |
| Fat | −80 to −120 |

**Windows to use:**
- **Brain window** (W80/L40): parenchyma assessment.
- **Subdural window** (W200/L50): extra-axial collections, subtle haemorrhage.
- **Bone window** (W2500/L500): distinguish calcification from blood; assess skull and sutures.

The single most useful habit: **always switch to bone windows when you are unsure whether something is calcification or haemorrhage.**

## The Developing Brain

### What Normal Looks Like — and Why It Can Fool You

In the neonate and young infant, white matter is largely unmyelinated and therefore **water-rich and low in attenuation (~15–20 HU)**. This means the normal grey–white contrast is reduced or even reversed compared with adults. Do not call this oedema.

Myelination progresses from brainstem outward and from deep to superficial. On CT:

- **Term neonate:** globally low-attenuation white matter; thalami, basal ganglia, and posterior limb of the internal capsule (PLIC) appear relatively brighter. Sulcation is appropriate for gestational age. Sutures and anterior fontanelle are open.
- **3–6 months:** improving but still reduced grey–white differentiation; posterior white matter brightening.
- **6–18 months:** progressive normalisation; approaching adult contrast by ~18–24 months.

**Only escalate for possible hypoxic–ischaemic injury if you also see:**
- Loss of expected grey–white differentiation *beyond* what age predicts
- Sulcal or ventricular effacement
- Abnormal deep grey (thalamic) hypodensity
- Basal cistern effacement

### Open Sutures and Fontanelles

Normal in neonates and infants. On CT they appear as hypodense linear gaps. **Do not mistake them for diastatic fractures.** Equally, beam-hardening artefact and partial volume at the skull base can mimic thin subdural collections — check in multiple planes and on subdural windows before reporting one.

### Benign Enlargement of Subarachnoid Spaces in Infancy (BESSI)

Seen in macrocephalic infants, typically peaking at 6–12 months. Caused by delayed maturation of arachnoid granulations.

**CT features:**
- Symmetric enlargement of frontoparietal subarachnoid spaces; CSF attenuation throughout.
- Cortical veins traverse the enlarged space rather than being displaced towards the cortex.
- Ventricles normal or mildly prominent; no mass effect.

**The critical distinction is from subdural hygromas or haematomas**, which are crescentic, may contain membranes, and displace cortical veins inward against the cortex. Misdiagnosing BESSI as a subdural collection can lead to unnecessary safeguarding escalation — look carefully for vein position and any internal structure.

**Document clearly:** BESSI carries an increased risk of subdural haemorrhage even after minor trauma, which is relevant context if the child re-presents.

## Normal Variants You Must Recognise

### Cavum Septum Pellucidum (CSP) and Cavum Vergae (CV)

A CSP is a CSF-attenuation (0–15 HU) midline space between the frontal horns, anterior to the foramina of Monro. CV is its posterior extension above the third ventricle. Both are well-defined, cause no mass effect, and are common incidental findings.

**Do not mistake for:** interhemispheric arachnoid cyst (tends to exert mass effect and may widen the callosal angle), an enlarged third ventricle (inferior location, associated hydrocephalus), or a midline cystic tumour.

**Action:** none required if isolated. No follow-up unless there is mass effect or associated callosal malformation.

### Asymmetric Lateral Ventricles

Up to a third of people have mild ventricular asymmetry, often left larger than right.

**Reassuring features:** smooth contours, no transependymal oedema, no midline shift, no focal obstructive point.

**Escalate if:** one frontal horn is ballooning, the septum pellucidum bows towards the smaller ventricle, or there are other features of raised intracranial pressure (ICP) — these suggest obstruction rather than normal variation.

### Mega Cisterna Magna

An enlarged retrocerebellar CSF space (>10 mm AP). The key is that the **vermis and fourth ventricle are normal** and there is no mass effect or hydrocephalus. It conforms to cisternal contours.

**Distinguish from:**
- **Arachnoid cyst:** may cause mass effect, scallop the adjacent bone, and compress the vermis or tonsils.
- **Dandy–Walker spectrum:** vermian hypoplasia or rotation, with an enlarged fourth ventricle communicating with the retrocerebellar space.

**Action:** incidental if isolated. No follow-up needed.

### Prominent Perivascular (Virchow–Robin) Spaces

CSF-attenuation foci following penetrating vessel trajectories. Typically round or linear, usually <3–5 mm. Common sites: basal ganglia (lenticulostriate distribution), high frontal centrum semiovale, and the mesencephalothalamic region.

**Do not label these as infarcts** in an asymptomatic patient. Small basal ganglia dots along vascular tracks are almost always perivascular spaces.

**Consider lacunes if:** the lesion is 3–15 mm, ovoid and isolated rather than in a linear cluster, in a patient with vascular risk factors or a prior lacunar syndrome. A CSF-density cavity in the right clinical context = chronic lacune.

**Action:** incidental. MRI if unusually large (>1.5 cm), asymmetric with mass effect, or atypical location.

## The Ageing Brain

### Age-Related Involutional Change

Expect widened sulci, prominent Sylvian fissures and basal cisterns, and symmetrically enlarged ventricles with **sharp margins and no periventricular oedema** — this is ex vacuo change, not hydrocephalus.

**The important differential is Normal Pressure Hydrocephalus (NPH):**
- Disproportionately enlarged ventricles relative to sulcal loss
- Tight high-convexity sulci with enlarged Sylvian fissures (the "DESH" pattern)
- May have periventricular low attenuation
- Clinical triad: gait disturbance, cognitive impairment, urinary symptoms

If you see this pattern, flag it explicitly for clinical correlation and consider recommending MRI and CSF dynamics assessment.

**Do not attribute focal sulcal effacement to atrophy** — look for an underlying mass, infarct, or oedema.

### Physiological Calcifications

These are common and should not be mistaken for haemorrhage. **Always confirm on bone windows** — calcification is extremely dense (often >150 HU, very bright on bone windows), whereas blood is less dense and softer-appearing.

Common sites and appearances:

- **Pineal gland:** punctate or lamellar, midline posterior to the third ventricle. Present in most adults by adolescence.
- **Choroid plexus:** coarse, bilateral, within the atria of the lateral ventricles.
- **Globus pallidus:** most common site of basal ganglia calcification; usually symmetric and fine. Thalamic and dentate calcification are less common.
- **Falx/tentorium:** linear or plaque-like; can be extensive; easily mistaken for acute subdural on narrow brain windows.

**When to escalate:** if basal ganglia calcification is extensive, asymmetric, or present under the age of 30, recommend metabolic screen (calcium, phosphate, PTH) and clinical correlation — consider hypoparathyroidism or Fahr disease.

## Common Background Pathology

### Chronic Small-Vessel Disease (SVD)

**CT features:**
- Periventricular low attenuation: "caps" at frontal horns and "bands" along lateral ventricles.
- Confluent deep white-matter hypodensity extending into the centrum semiovale.
- Symmetric, bilateral; U-fibres spared until advanced disease.
- No acute mass effect.

**Distinguish from acute infarction:** acute infarction involves cortical territory, causes gyral swelling and sulcal effacement, is asymmetric, and respects a vascular distribution.

**Why it matters in the ED:** Extensive SVD increases the risk of haemorrhagic transformation if thrombolysis is given. **Always state the severity** (mild/moderate/severe) in your acute stroke report.

### Lacunar Infarcts (Chronic)

Round or ovoid CSF-attenuation cavities (0–15 HU), typically 3–15 mm, in the basal ganglia, thalamus, internal capsule, centrum semiovale, or pons. A CSF-density cavity here = **chronic**, not acute.

**Acute lacunar infarct is often CT-occult** — the lesion may be isodense or subtly hypodense with no cavitation. If you suspect an acute lacunar syndrome and CT is normal or equivocal, MRI diffusion is needed.

Document number and location of chronic lacunes; they inform vascular risk management.

### Meningiomas

**CT features:**
- Extra-axial, iso- to hyperdense mass (40–70 HU), often with coarse or psammomatous calcification (>150 HU).
- Broad dural base, obtuse angle with skull, CSF cleft between mass and brain, cortical buckling.
- Bone windows: hyperostosis and radiating vascular grooves ("sunburst").
- En plaque variant: sheet-like dural thickening with marked adjacent hyperostosis and minimal intradural component — easily missed.

**Distinguish from parafalcine subdural haemorrhage:** meningioma is focal, may be calcified, causes cortical buckling, and has adjacent hyperostosis rather than a crescentic spread of blood.

**When to escalate:**
- Symptomatic, large (>3 cm), with surrounding oedema or significant mass effect → **urgent MRI and neurosurgical review**.
- Atypical calcification or adjacent bone erosion → MRI for characterisation.
- Small, densely calcified, incidental, and stable → document and suggest routine outpatient consideration.

### Pineal Cysts

Well-circumscribed, round, CSF-attenuation lesion in the pineal region, often with **peripheral rim calcification** (the pineal calcification is displaced outward by the cyst — a reassuring sign). No solid component, no hydrocephalus.

**Distinguish from pineal region tumours** (pineocytoma, pineoblastoma, germinoma): these are solid or mixed, enhance, may engulf rather than displace calcification, and can obstruct the aqueduct causing hydrocephalus.

**Action:**
- Typical cyst ≤10–15 mm, no hydrocephalus → incidental, no urgent action.
- Atypical (wall nodularity, haemorrhage) or causing hydrocephalus → MRI and neurosurgical referral.

### Arachnoid Cysts

Extra-axial, sharply demarcated, CSF-attenuation. No internal septa, no enhancement. May remodel/scallop adjacent bone. Common in the middle cranial fossa and posterior fossa.

**In the trauma setting:** arachnoid cysts predispose to subdural haemorrhage — scrutinise carefully for acute blood layering over or adjacent to the cyst. A stable, pre-existing cyst is not itself the emergency, but do not let it distract you from an overlying acute collection.

**Distinguish from epidermoid:** slightly above CSF attenuation on CT, insinuates around cisternal structures, DWI-bright on MRI.

**Action:** incidental unless causing symptomatic mass effect or hydrocephalus. MRI if atypical density or internal complexity.

### Cerebellar Tonsillar Descent

CT has limited soft-tissue resolution for this. On sagittal reconstructions, the tonsils may appear at or just below the foramen magnum. **Do not call low-lying tonsils herniation** unless there is accompanying supratentorial mass effect and cisternal effacement.

Chiari I is defined as ≥5 mm tonsillar descent in adults **with symptoms** (occipital headache worsened by Valsalva, brainstem or cerebellar signs). CT cannot reliably assess posterior fossa crowding or syrinx.

**Action:** if tonsils appear more than minimally low-lying and/or the patient has relevant symptoms, recommend MRI of the craniovertebral junction and whole spine to assess for syrinx.

## Practical Pattern Recognition: Acute vs Chronic vs Normal

| Appearance | Likely interpretation |
|---|---|
| CSF-density ovoid cavity in basal ganglia/thalamus | Chronic lacune — not acute |
| Very high HU, crisp on bone windows | Calcification — not blood |
| Extra-axial mass with hyperostosis and CSF cleft | Meningioma — not haemorrhage |
| Symmetric periventricular low attenuation, no mass effect | Chronic SVD — not acute oedema |
| Anterior subarachnoid enlargement in infant, veins traversing space | BESSI — not subdural collection |
| Low-attenuation white matter in neonate, sutures open | Normal for age — not oedema or fracture |

## Imaging Pitfalls — Quick Reference

- **Neonatal brain:** unmyelinated white matter ≠ diffuse oedema; patent sutures ≠ diastatic fractures.
- **Calcification vs blood:** always check bone windows; calcification is far denser and crisper.
- **Falx calcification:** linear, midline, very dense — not a subdural haematoma.
- **Pineal/choroid calcification:** not intraventricular haemorrhage — confirm on bone windows.
- **SVD in stroke:** document severity; affects thrombolysis risk–benefit.
- **Perivascular spaces:** do not label as "multiple infarcts" in an asymptomatic patient.
- **BESSI vs subdural:** check for internal membranes and cortical vein displacement.
- **En plaque meningioma:** do not dismiss as dural thickening — look for subtle soft tissue and adjacent hyperostosis.
- **Low-lying tonsils:** not herniation without global mass effect.

## Summary Checklist for Every Acute Brain CT

1. **Patient age** — calibrate expectations for myelination, sutures, and physiological calcification.
2. **Apply bone windows** — confirm calcification vs blood wherever uncertain.
3. **Extra-axial vs intra-axial** — CSF cleft, dural base, hyperostosis = likely meningioma.
4. **White matter low attenuation** — symmetric and periventricular = chronic SVD; territorial with cortical swelling = acute infarct.
5. **Deep cavities** — CSF-density ovoid = chronic lacune; linear/clustered along vessels = perivascular spaces.
6. **Posterior fossa CSF** — normal vermis and fourth ventricle = mega cisterna magna; vermian abnormality or enlarged fourth ventricle = consider Dandy–Walker.
7. **Ventricles** — mild asymmetry is common; look for an obstructive point and periventricular oedema before calling hydrocephalus.
8. **Pineal region** — CSF-density lesion with peripheral calcification = pineal cyst; check for aqueduct obstruction.
9. **Infants** — symmetric anterior subarachnoid spaces with traversing veins = BESSI; check vein position
